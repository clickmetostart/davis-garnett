<p><em>Rob Miller, Branch Manager at MadCity Home Loans, explains the appraisal gap — one of the trickiest situations a Wisconsin buyer can face in a hot market.</em></p>

<h3>What is an appraisal gap and how do I handle it?</h3>

<p>
  An appraisal gap occurs when the home you're purchasing appraises for <em>less</em> than the price you agreed to pay. For example: you offered $400,000, but the appraiser says the home is worth $382,000. That $18,000 difference is the appraisal gap.
</p>

<h3>Why It Matters for Your Mortgage</h3>
<p>
  Your lender can only loan against the <strong>lower of the purchase price or the appraised value</strong>. So if you're putting 5% down on a $400,000 home ($20,000), and the home appraises at $382,000, your lender now calculates your loan on $382,000 — meaning your $20,000 covers less of the gap and you'd need to bring additional cash to close.
</p>

<h3>Your Options</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Cover the gap in cash:</strong> Bring additional funds to close to cover the difference between the appraised value and the purchase price. Requires strong reserves.</li>
  <li><strong>Renegotiate the price:</strong> Ask the seller to reduce the price to the appraised value, or split the gap. Common in balanced markets.</li>
  <li><strong>Appraisal Reconsideration of Value (ROV):</strong> If you have strong comps the appraiser missed, we can challenge the appraisal through a formal ROV process.</li>
  <li><strong>Walk away:</strong> If you have an appraisal contingency in your contract, you can exit and recover your earnest money.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Appraisal Gap Coverage Clause</h3>
<p>
  In competitive markets, buyers sometimes include an "appraisal gap coverage clause" in their offer — committing to cover a specified amount of any gap (e.g., "buyer will cover up to $15,000 of any appraisal gap"). This can make your offer more attractive without removing the appraisal contingency entirely.
</p>
