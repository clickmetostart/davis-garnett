<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Your credit score is one of the most important numbers in your financial life — especially when you're preparing to buy a home. At MadCity Home Loans powered by ProVisor, Inc., Rob Miller and his team work with Wisconsin home buyers at every credit level. Whether you're sitting at 580 or 780, understanding your credit and knowing how to improve it puts you in a much stronger position when it's time to apply for a mortgage.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This guide covers everything you need to know to boost and maintain your credit score before approaching a lender.</p>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why Your Credit Score Matters for a Home Loan</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Your credit score tells lenders how reliably you've managed borrowed money in the past. A higher score unlocks real financial advantages when it comes to your mortgage:</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li><strong>Larger loan amounts</strong> — a stronger credit profile increases how much you can borrow</li>
  <li><strong>Lower interest rates</strong> — even a half-point rate difference can save you tens of thousands over the life of a loan</li>
  <li><strong>Faster, easier approval</strong> — a clean credit history means less friction in underwriting</li>
  <li><strong>Better negotiating power</strong> — sellers and lenders take strong-credit buyers more seriously</li>
</ul>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That said, you may be surprised to learn that you don't need perfect credit to qualify for a home loan. Rob Miller has helped Wisconsin buyers with credit scores in the low-to-mid 600s secure financing — often through FHA, VA, or <a href="/knowledge-hub/wheda-loans-wisconsin-down-payment-assistance">WHEDA</a> programs that have more flexible standards. The goal isn't perfection. The goal is improvement — and knowing where you stand so we can build the right strategy together.</p>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 1: Check Your Credit Report for Errors</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before anything else, pull your credit reports from all three major bureaus: <strong>Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion</strong>. You're entitled to a free report from each at annualcreditreport.com.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What to look for:</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li>Accounts you don't recognize (possible identity theft or fraud)</li>
  <li>Payments marked late that you know you paid on time</li>
  <li>Debts that show incorrect balances</li>
  <li>Accounts that should have been closed or removed</li>
</ul>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If you find errors, dispute them immediately with the reporting bureau. Credit bureau disputes are handled in writing and typically resolved within 30–45 days. Removing a legitimate error can have a meaningful positive impact on your score.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Important note:</strong> Checking your own credit is a <em>soft inquiry</em> — it does not affect your score. Don't be afraid to look.</p>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 2: Pay Your Bills On Time, Every Time</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Payment history is the single largest factor in your credit score — typically accounting for about 35% of your FICO score. One late payment can drag your score down significantly, especially if it's on a credit card or loan account.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Key points:</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li>Credit card and loan payments have the biggest impact</li>
  <li>Utility bills (electric, gas, water) generally don't affect your credit unless they go to collections</li>
  <li>Set up autopay for at least the <strong>minimum payment</strong> on every credit account — this protects you from accidental late payments even if you plan to pay more manually</li>
</ul>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If you've had late payments in the past, the good news is that their impact fades over time. Twelve to twenty-four months of consistent on-time payments can significantly offset older negative marks.</p>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 3: Understand Your Credit Utilization Ratio</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Your <strong>credit utilization ratio</strong> is the percentage of your total available credit that you're currently using. It's the second-largest factor in your score — and one of the fastest to improve.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>The formula:</strong> Total debt owed ÷ Total available credit = Credit utilization</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here's a quick example:</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li>You have two credit cards with a combined limit of $10,000</li>
  <li>You currently carry $1,500 in balances</li>
  <li>Your utilization ratio is 15% — which is excellent</li>
</ul>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For a healthy credit score, aim to keep your utilization below <strong>30%</strong>. If you can get it below 10%, even better. Rob Miller's team consistently sees score improvements when buyers pay down revolving balances before applying for a mortgage.</p>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 4: Don't Close Unused Credit Cards</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is one of the most common credit mistakes Rob sees from buyers preparing for a home purchase. When you close a credit card account, you reduce your total available credit — which instantly raises your utilization ratio even if your debt stays the same.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For example: if you carry $2,000 in debt against $10,000 in available credit, your utilization is 20%. Close a card with a $4,000 limit and suddenly that same $2,000 debt is against only $6,000 in available credit — bumping your ratio to 33%.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>The exception:</strong> if a card has an annual fee you're not using, weigh the cost against the score benefit. In most cases, keeping the card open with a $0 balance and setting a small recurring charge (like a streaming subscription) on autopay is the right move.</p>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 5: Keep Credit Card Spending Low</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Aim to keep your spending on any individual credit card below <strong>15–20% of that card's limit</strong> before your payment posts. Credit bureaus look at your reported balances — not just whether you pay in full each month.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A practical strategy:</p>

<ul class="wp-block-list">
  <li>Use credit cards for fixed, predictable expenses you already budget for (gas, groceries)</li>
  <li>Avoid using credit cards for variable "want" spending that can creep up unexpectedly</li>
  <li>Pay the balance down before the statement closing date, not just the due date</li>
</ul>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Step 6: Avoid Opening New Credit Before Applying</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Every time you apply for a new credit account, it triggers a <strong>hard inquiry</strong> on your report — which can temporarily lower your score by a few points. More importantly, new accounts lower your average account age, which also factors into your score.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Rob Miller's guidance: avoid opening any new credit accounts in the <strong>six months</strong> before applying for a mortgage. That includes new credit cards, auto loans, furniture financing, and "buy now, pay later" accounts.</p>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Your Credit Score Roadmap</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here's a simple summary of the actions that move the needle most:</p>

<ol class="wp-block-list">
  <li><strong>Check your credit reports</strong> for errors — dispute anything inaccurate</li>
  <li><strong>Pay bills on time</strong> — set autopay for minimums on all accounts</li>
  <li><strong>Keep utilization below 30%</strong> — ideally below 10% before applying</li>
  <li><strong>Don't close unused credit cards</strong> — keep available credit high</li>
  <li><strong>Keep card spending low</strong> — pay down balances before statement dates</li>
  <li><strong>Avoid new credit applications</strong> — especially in the six months before your mortgage</li>
</ol>

<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity"/>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading">You Don't Need Perfect Credit to Buy a Home</h2>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the most important things Rob Miller wants Wisconsin buyers to understand: <strong>you may already qualify — and just don't know it.</strong></p>

And WHEDA loan programs in Wisconsin are designed specifically to help buyers who don't fit the conventional lending mold.</p>
